
## 1. Reactions leave a trace

The current implementation explicitly forces slow reactions to finish at zero. That works against your idea.

Instead, reaction consumes only the amount above a small residual concentration `ε`:

```text
reactable red   = max(0, r - ε)
reactable green = max(0, g - ε)
```

Apply the reaction law to those amounts. This preserves existing traces without creating pigment in cells that have none.

**A trace alone is not a gradient.** Green still needs to diffuse across the border, while reaction consumes it away from the border. Together these can maintain the weak green signal inside red territory.

Fixed-point rounding matters here: very small transport must accumulate rather than disappear every step.

## 2. Influence means local, directional transport

Red’s response to green can be expressed as:

```text
red flux = -Dᵣ ∇r + χᵣg · r · ∇g
```

- `-Dᵣ ∇r`: ordinary spreading.
- Positive `χᵣg`: red moves toward higher green concentration.
- Zero: red ignores green.
- Negative: red moves away from green.

Compute this only across neighboring cell edges, transferring existing red mass conservatively. **Nothing searches for the border.** The border becomes apparent through the gradient.

The responses should be directional: red’s attraction to green and green’s attraction to red are separate player settings.

This gives your example: green traces enter red territory → red follows the local gradient → red concentrates near the front → pressure, mixing, and reaction determine whether green retreats. Attraction should not itself guarantee victory.

## 3. Every cell can produce pigment

Using normalized concentrations, where `1` corresponds to the usual `255` level, define:

```text
s = r + g + b
```

Expose your three source rules as experimental modes:

| Mode | Red production rate |
|---|---|
| Dominance | `α` when `r > k·(g+b)`, otherwise `0` |
| Low pigment | `α · f(max(0, 3-s))` |
| Low pigment + threshold | Same, but only when `s < T` |

A simple first choice is `f(x) = x/3`, so production remains between `0` and `α` when `s` is between `0` and `3`.

There is one important choice in the last two modes:

**Can an entirely empty cell create every color?**

- **Yes:** all three pigments grow even in empty regions. This is spontaneous background production.
- **No:** multiply each color’s production by its own presence, for example `r/(r+h)` for red. Empty cells stay empty, but even a small red trace can grow.

The second option feels closer to your living-tissue analogy: pigment spreads and locally reproduces, rather than appearing everywhere independently. But it changes your literal formula, so I would make that choice explicit rather than silently add it.

My recommendation for the first experiment: **trace-preserving reaction + directional gradient response + these three selectable production laws**, without hubs or new economy mechanics.

For the low-pigment modes, do you want **spontaneous production in empty cells**, or **growth only where that pigment already exists**?

## Production and control

Normalize pigment concentrations so `1 = 255`, and use `ε = 0.05`.

For red, all production modes require:

```text
r ≥ ε
```

Then apply the selected rule:

```text
dominance:     r > k·(g+b)
low pigment:   f(max(0, 3-(r+g+b)))
thresholded:  same, only when r+g+b < T
```

Production should also slow as red approaches its local ceiling, rather than abruptly clipping:

```text
red production = rate · eligibility · fertility · max(0, 1-r/C)
```

Here `C` is red’s local concentration ceiling; `fertility = 1` for dominance mode.

A cell with **all three pigments below 5% is uncontrolled**. A cell where several exceed 5% can be mixed or contested; exceeding the threshold alone does not imply exclusive ownership.

## Reaction: slow naturally, without a hard residual floor

Your proposal is better than my earlier fixed `ε` remainder: **reaction should approach zero smoothly, not stop at a preset concentration.**

A useful starting law for mutual consumption is:

```text
m = min(a, b)
reaction amount = λ · m · a · b
```

With normalized concentrations and `λ = 0.01…0.02`:

| Concentrations | Minority consumed per tick |
|---|---:|
| `a=1, b=1` | 1–2% |
| `a=1, b=0.1` | 0.1–0.2% |
| `a=1, b=0.01` | 0.01–0.02% |

This means strong overlap reacts most, while a thin invading trace survives much longer.

Your `a²b²` idea suppresses traces even more strongly. We can expose an exponent for experimentation:

```text
reaction amount = λ · min(a,b) · (a·b)^n

n = 1: gentler suppression
n = 2: much longer-lived traces
```

Clamp the normalized factors to `0…1` so concentrations above the reference level cannot produce unexpectedly explosive reaction rates.

**I would start with `n=1`, `λ=2%`.** Then compare `n=2`: too much suppression may let traces spread almost uniformly, weakening the useful gradient.

Diffusion supplies rival pigment from the border; concentration-dependent reaction consumes it along the way. Their balance creates the gradient—without searching for a border or measuring distance.

Implementation detail: remove the existing minimum-one-quantum reaction rule and **accumulate fractional reaction amounts**, otherwise tiny reactions either disappear numerically or are forced to consume traces too quickly.

The resulting distinction is important: **5% governs control and production only; transport and reaction remain continuous below it.**
